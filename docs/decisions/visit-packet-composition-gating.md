## Question

For the user-controlled clinician Visit Packet (Home pill `prep`):
1. **Composition** — what is included in a packet, and how is every claim source-linked back to its evidence (C5) so there are no orphan claims?
2. **Scoped share/export + revocation** (C1) — what is the grant model for sharing/exporting a packet (audience, purpose, duration), and what are the exact revocation semantics?
3. **C10 gating** — how does the generated summary pass the Safety & Governance Gate (do-not-diagnose, panic-language, provenance, bias) before it can be shared?

## Context

Touches C7 (Health Thread Engine), C5 (Evidence & Provenance), C10 (Safety & Governance Gate), C1 (Trust & Consent) — see `docs/architecture/component-map.md`. The packet is the first WellBe output that leaves the personal core for a third party, so a wrong decision on share scoping, revocation, or C10 gating is a safety/privacy regression that is expensive or impossible to reverse once a packet has been shared externally. Personal-first and grant-scoped guardrails apply (`.cursor/rules/wellbe-vision-guardrails.mdc`).

## 1. External patterns to examine

### A. Packet content / source-linked claims — C7 + C5

- **Patient-summary document pattern:** HL7’s International Patient Summary (IPS) frames a patient summary as a **minimal, non-exhaustive, clinically relevant snapshot** of essential health information, originally for unplanned cross-border care but also usable for planned/local care. IPS explicitly treats the summary as a FHIR **Bundle** organized through a **Composition** resource. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/))  
- **Minimum clinical sections pattern:** IPS identifies current **allergies/intolerances, medications, and problems** as the minimum structural expectation for all IPS documents, while allowing other sections when relevant. It also recognizes that not all lifetime data is relevant at summary-generation time. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Generation-and-Data-Inclusion.html))  
- **Empty / unavailable data pattern:** IPS distinguishes between omitted optional sections and required sections where data is missing. For required sections, it uses `Composition.section.emptyReason` or explicit “known absence” statements rather than leaving ambiguity. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Empty-Sections-and-Missing-Data.html))  
- **Narrative plus structured-entry pattern:** IPS requires human-readable section text for populated sections and uses `Composition.section.entry` references to structured resources where available. FHIR Composition also permits narrative-only sections, but the IPS pattern favors entries where data exists. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Empty-Sections-and-Missing-Data.html))  
- **Provenance / no-orphan-claim pattern:** FHIR Provenance records the “who, what, when” behind resources and the entities/processes involved in producing them; it supports assessments of authenticity, trust, reliability, and reproducibility. The Provenance resource can point to any generated or updated resource and can reference source entities using roles such as `source`, `derivation`, `quotation`, or `revision`. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/provenance.html))  
- **Document metadata pattern:** FHIR DocumentReference separates metadata about a document from the document itself and explicitly notes that there can be two provenance layers: provenance of the referenced document and provenance of the DocumentReference record. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/DocumentReference.html))  
- **Patient-generated data pattern:** HL7’s draft Personal Health Record / PGHD guidance describes patient-generated health data as data created, recorded, or gathered by patients or caregivers outside clinical settings, and maps such data to FHIR resources such as Observation and related resources. It is a continuous build, so its stability is lower than published normative/stable standards. ([build.fhir.org](https://build.fhir.org/ig/HL7/personal-health-record-format-ig/en/pghd.html?utm_source=openai))  

### B. Scoped share/export + revocation — C1 + C5

- **Consent-grant structure pattern:** FHIR Consent models healthcare consent directives with policy context, grantor/grantee concepts, provisions, actors, purposes of use, data objects, date ranges, and references back to the source consent directive. It also states that enforcement is outside the Consent resource and typically depends on access-control mechanisms such as OAuth, UMA, XACML, RBAC, or ABAC. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/r4/Consent.html))  
- **HIPAA authorization-element pattern:** U.S. HIPAA authorization rules require, among other elements, a specific description of information disclosed, who may disclose, who may receive, purpose, expiration date/event, signature/date, revocation notice, and a redisclosure warning; the authorization must be in plain language. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.508))  
- **Revocation semantics pattern:** HIPAA permits revocation of an authorization in writing, except to the extent the covered entity has already acted in reliance on it. This establishes a common legal pattern: revocation can stop future reliance/disclosure, but it does not automatically unwind prior disclosures. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.508))  
- **Defective / inactive grant pattern:** HIPAA treats authorizations as invalid if the expiration date/event has passed, required elements are incomplete, the authorization is known to have been revoked, or material information is false. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.508))  
- **FHIR security-label / purpose-of-use pattern:** FHIR security labels can express confidentiality, sensitivity, purpose of use, and handling caveats on resources or bundles. The specification states that labels derive meaning from a broader policy/consent framework and that recipient enforcement depends on the applicable trust framework. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/R4/security-labels.html?utm_source=openai))  
- **SMART scoped-token pattern:** SMART App Launch uses OAuth scopes to request specific FHIR resource permissions; the specification cautions that wildcard scopes can expose more data than needed and encourages clients to request only the permissions they need. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/smart-app-launch/1.0.0/scopes-and-launch-context/index.html?utm_source=openai))  
- **SMART Health Links pattern:** SMART Health Links lets a sharing user choose what to share, whether to require a passcode, and whether the link expires. The protocol supports FHIR JSON payloads, passcode-protected links, expiration hints, encrypted files, short-lived file URLs, and a server response of `404` when a link is no longer active. ([docs.smarthealthit.org](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/spec/?utm_source=openai))  
- **Export-boundary pattern:** HHS guidance on patient access, apps, and APIs says that once health information is transmitted to a third-party app at the individual’s direction, the covered entity is generally not liable under HIPAA for the app’s subsequent use/disclosure; HHS also notes that health information entered into a non-HIPAA-regulated mobile app is generally not PHI under HIPAA. ([hhs.gov](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/access-right-health-apps-apis/index.html?utm_source=openai))  

### C. Safety gate for generated summaries — C10 + C5 + C7

- **Non-diagnostic / no-directive pattern:** FDA’s CDS guidance distinguishes software that provides information/options from software that gives specific preventive, diagnostic, or treatment outputs or directives. It also flags specific follow-up directives, time-critical alarms, treatment plans, and risk scores as examples that do not satisfy the “recommendations rather than directives” criterion. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/step-6-software-function-intended-provide-clinical-decision-support))  
- **Basis-for-review pattern:** FDA’s CDS guidance says software intended to let healthcare professionals independently review the basis of recommendations should identify intended use, required inputs, relevance/data-quality requirements, algorithm logic/methods, data relied upon, validation information, and patient-specific knowns/unknowns. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/step-6-software-function-intended-provide-clinical-decision-support))  
- **AI governance pattern:** WHO’s AI-for-health principles include human autonomy, safety, transparency/explainability/intelligibility, accountability, inclusiveness/equity, and responsive/sustainable use. WHO specifically warns about bias, patient-safety risks, cybersecurity risks, and overestimating AI benefits. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-06-2021-who-issues-first-global-report-on-ai-in-health-and-six-guiding-principles-for-its-design-and-use))  
- **AI trustworthiness pattern:** NIST’s AI RMF frames trustworthy AI characteristics as valid/reliable, safe, secure/resilient, accountable/transparent, explainable/interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed. NIST also notes that tradeoffs among trustworthiness characteristics are common. ([nist.gov](https://www.nist.gov/node/1674681))  
- **Plain-language / calm communication pattern:** CDC health-literacy guidance says health information should be accurate, accessible, and actionable, and that plain-language practices are not “dumbing down” or distorting the science. ([cdc.gov](https://www.cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/develop-test-materials.html))  
- **Patient appointment-prep pattern:** AHRQ’s QuestionBuilder helps patients and caregivers prepare questions for medical visits, organize appointment-specific concerns, and keep data under user control; its FAQ notes that data entered in the app stays on the user’s device unless the user uses email/calendar features, in which case those providers may store copies. ([ahrq.gov](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/question-builder/index.html?utm_source=openai))  
- **Patient-authored pre-visit narrative pattern:** OpenNotes’ OurNotes asks patients to compose interval history, goals, and questions before a visit, using the “subjective” component of SOAP as a familiar clinical-communication pattern while keeping the patient contribution distinct from clinician-authored documentation. ([opennotes.org](https://www.opennotes.org/ournotes/?utm_source=openai))

## 3. Decision-neutral findings mapped to the decision questions and affected components

### Decision question 1: What is included in a packet and how is each claim source-linked so there are no orphan claims?

**Affected components:** C7 Health Thread Engine, C5 Evidence & Provenance, C10 Safety & Governance Gate.

- **External patient-summary standards separate “minimum core” from “relevant additional data.”** IPS treats the patient summary as minimal and non-exhaustive, with allergies/intolerances, medications, and problems as minimum structural expectations; it allows additional clinically relevant sections and recognizes that summary-generation may filter lifetime records for relevance. This maps to C7’s thread-to-packet composition question because the packet can be compared against established “core + relevant extras” summary patterns without treating IPS as a required product shape. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/))  

- **Clinical-summary standards preserve absence semantics rather than silently omitting required concepts.** IPS uses `emptyReason`, data-absent-reason extensions, and “known absence” patterns when data is unavailable, not asked, masked, or known absent. This maps to C5 because “no orphan claims” also includes avoiding implied claims from missing sections, such as implying “no allergy” when the real state is “not asked” or “unavailable.” ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Empty-Sections-and-Missing-Data.html))  

- **FHIR Composition supports sectioned documents with authorship, attestation, custodian, subject, and section entries.** Composition can contain narrative-only text, but IPS adds expectations for section text and structured entries where populated. This creates an external distinction between a human-readable summary and the underlying referenced structured resources. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Empty-Sections-and-Missing-Data.html))  

- **FHIR Provenance supports resource-level source chains but does not by itself define claim-level citation UX.** Provenance can identify targets, agents, entities used, activity, timestamps, and roles such as `source`, `quotation`, `derivation`, and `revision`. The standard provides the machinery for traceability, while the claim-level rule “every generated sentence/claim must map to raw source” remains an implementation-level contract above base FHIR. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/provenance.html))  

- **DocumentReference introduces a separate provenance layer for the packet artifact.** A generated packet may have provenance for the source data used inside it and separate provenance for the packet document/reference itself. This matters to C5 because a packet can be “generated from” raw sources while its exported PDF/link/document also has authorship, creation time, status, and custodian metadata. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/DocumentReference.html))  

- **Patient-generated data is recognized as a distinct source class.** HL7 PGHD guidance defines PGHD as patient/caregiver-created or gathered data outside clinical settings, including logs, self-measured vitals, diet records, and symptom diaries. This maps to WellBe’s personal-first model because source labels may need to distinguish patient-authored statements, device readings, imported clinical records, AI-derived summaries, and transformed/normalized records. ([build.fhir.org](https://build.fhir.org/ig/HL7/personal-health-record-format-ig/en/pghd.html?utm_source=openai))  

- **Appointment-prep prior art emphasizes user questions, goals, symptoms, medicines, and concerns.** AHRQ’s patient-prep materials and QuestionBuilder focus on questions for the visit, symptoms/health history, medications, tests, procedures, and user-controlled sharing; OpenNotes OurNotes focuses on interval history, goals, and visit questions. These patterns are narrower than a clinical patient summary but relevant to a visit packet’s non-diagnostic, patient-to-clinician handoff role. ([ahrq.gov](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/question-builder/index.html?utm_source=openai))  

### Decision question 2: What is the scoped share/export + revocation model?

**Affected components:** C1 Trust & Consent, C5 Evidence & Provenance, C10 Safety & Governance Gate.

- **External consent models commonly encode audience/recipient, purpose, duration, data scope, and source consent reference.** HIPAA authorization requires recipient, purpose, information scope, expiration, revocation notice, redisclosure warning, and plain language; FHIR Consent supports provisions for recipients/actors, purpose-of-use, data references, date ranges, and source-consent references. These map directly to C1 grant-shape fields. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.508))  

- **Revocation has two separate external meanings: access revocation and copy recall.** HIPAA’s authorization revocation stops future reliance except where the covered entity has already acted in reliance, which indicates that legal revocation does not necessarily invalidate prior disclosures. SMART Health Links can make a link inactive so future manifest requests fail, but the protocol does not by itself erase data already fetched or copied by a recipient. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.508))  

- **FHIR Consent explicitly separates consent representation from enforcement.** The FHIR Consent resource can express consent directives or metadata, but its text says enforcement is expected through other access-control methodologies. For C1, this means the grant object and the enforcement mechanism are externally recognized as separable layers. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/r4/Consent.html))  

- **SMART Health Links provide a concrete external pattern for time-boxed, passcode-protected link sharing.** The SHL payload includes a manifest URL, decryption key, optional expiration hint, passcode-required flag, label, and version; manifest responses can include FHIR JSON, and file locations are short-lived and may be one-time-use. If the SHLink is no longer active, the resource server returns `404`. ([docs.smarthealthit.org](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/spec/?utm_source=openai))  

- **FHIR security labels and purpose-of-use codes can carry handling context but rely on downstream agreement.** FHIR labels can express purpose-of-use, confidentiality, and handling caveats; the specification says labels enable policy fragments to accompany data but are meaningful only within a wider security/trust framework. This maps to C1/C5 as metadata that can travel with the packet but not guarantee recipient behavior after export. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/R4/security-labels.html?utm_source=openai))  

- **SMART scopes show a least-permission pattern for API grants.** SMART warns that broad wildcard scopes can authorize more future data than the user expects and encourages requesting only needed scopes. This maps to packet-generation/export grants as an external analogue for exact scope boundaries. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/smart-app-launch/1.0.0/scopes-and-launch-context/index.html?utm_source=openai))  

- **User-controlled sharing prior art includes explicit copy-boundary warnings.** AHRQ’s QuestionBuilder FAQ says user-entered data remains on-device unless the user uses email/calendar features, in which case those account providers may store copies. HHS guidance similarly distinguishes a covered entity’s responsibilities from third-party app behavior after patient-directed transmission. These sources support treating “share within controlled link” and “export/copy into third-party systems” as distinct states. ([ahrq.gov](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/question-builder/faqs.html?utm_source=openai))  

### Decision question 3: How does generated summary text pass C10 before it can be shared?

**Affected components:** C10 Safety & Governance Gate, C5 Evidence & Provenance, C7 Health Thread Engine, C1 Trust & Consent.

- **External CDS guidance distinguishes summaries/options from diagnostic or treatment directives.** FDA’s CDS materials include patient data reports/summaries as examples in the CDS context, but distinguish these from specific preventive, diagnostic, or treatment outputs/directives, time-critical alarms, treatment plans, follow-up directives, and disease risk scores. This maps to C10’s do-not-diagnose and never-alarm checks. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/step-6-software-function-intended-provide-clinical-decision-support))  

- **FDA’s “basis for independent review” pattern aligns with source-linked summaries.** FDA describes independent review support as including input information, data quality requirements, algorithm logic/methods, data relied upon, validation information, and patient-specific knowns/unknowns. For C10/C5, this is external support for gating generated text against the evidence it relied on, rather than presenting unsupported conclusions. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/step-6-software-function-intended-provide-clinical-decision-support))  

- **WHO and NIST both identify transparency, safety, accountability, bias/fairness, and human control as AI health risk controls.** WHO emphasizes human control of health-care systems and medical decisions, transparency/explainability, accountability, inclusiveness/equity, and patient-safety risks; NIST lists trustworthy AI characteristics including validity/reliability, safety, transparency/accountability, explainability, privacy, and harmful-bias management. These map to C10 categories for safety, provenance, and bias. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-06-2021-who-issues-first-global-report-on-ai-in-health-and-six-guiding-principles-for-its-design-and-use))  

- **CDC health-literacy guidance supports calm, plain, non-distorting language.** CDC frames effective health information as accurate, accessible, and actionable, and states that plain language should preserve scientific accuracy rather than distort it. This maps to C10 checks for calm language, non-alarmism, and comprehensibility. ([cdc.gov](https://www.cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/develop-test-materials.html))  

- **Patient-prep patterns keep the patient contribution distinct from clinician diagnosis.** AHRQ QuestionBuilder focuses on patient questions and preparation, while OurNotes asks patients to provide interval history, goals, and questions. Neither pattern turns patient-authored prep material into a clinician diagnosis. This supports a safety distinction between “what I noticed / what I want to ask / what sources show” and “what condition I have / what treatment I need.” ([ahrq.gov](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/question-builder/index.html?utm_source=openai))  

- **Provenance gating has both raw-source and generated-artifact layers.** FHIR Provenance can record source entities and generating agents for resources, while DocumentReference can represent the generated packet document and its metadata. For C10, this means gate results could be associated with the generated packet artifact, while C5 claim links can remain attached to underlying source records. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/provenance.html))

## 4. Tradeoffs and open questions

### A. Packet inclusion scope

- **Option space:** A packet could mirror IPS-like core sections; focus narrowly on visit-prep questions/goals/symptoms; or combine a patient-prep layer with a source-backed health-summary layer. External standards show all three ingredients exist, but they do not define the exact WellBe packet boundary. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/))  
- **Risk if too broad:** Larger packets may include stale, irrelevant, or overly sensitive data; IPS notes that not all lifetime data is relevant at summary-generation time. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Generation-and-Data-Inclusion.html))  
- **Risk if too narrow:** A packet that omits medications, allergies, or problems may diverge from common patient-summary expectations, even if it remains useful as a visit-prep artifact. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Generation-and-Data-Inclusion.html))  
- **Open question:** Whether “required” packet fields mean “must include if known,” “must include with absence reason,” or “must be user-selectable but visibly omitted if deselected.” IPS provides missing-data mechanics but not this product policy. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Empty-Sections-and-Missing-Data.html))  

### B. Claim-level provenance granularity

- **Option space:** Provenance can be tracked at packet level, section level, claim/sentence level, or source-resource level. FHIR Provenance supports resource-level provenance and source entities, but claim-level citation is an application-layer choice. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/provenance.html))  
- **Risk if provenance is only packet-level:** A clinician or user may not be able to tell which raw source supports a specific generated statement. This would weaken the “no orphan claims” guardrail even if the packet has document-level provenance. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/provenance.html))  
- **Risk if provenance is claim-level everywhere:** Fine-grained citation can increase implementation complexity, UI density, and failure modes when sources conflict or when one sentence summarizes many sources. FHIR does not prescribe a claim-level UX. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/provenance.html))  
- **Open question:** How to represent generated inferences versus direct quotations, since FHIR Provenance has entity roles such as `quotation`, `source`, and `derivation` but does not decide product semantics for “AI-derived from source.” ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/R4/provenance.html))  

### C. Missing data and known absence

- **Option space:** Missing data can be omitted, marked unavailable/not asked/masked, or represented as a known-absence clinical statement. IPS uses different mechanisms depending on requiredness, coding, and data type. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Empty-Sections-and-Missing-Data.html))  
- **Risk if omitted silently:** The packet may imply that no issue exists when the source state is actually unknown or unavailable. ([hl7.org](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/Empty-Sections-and-Missing-Data.html))  
- **Risk if every unknown is surfaced:** The packet may become noisy or anxiety-producing, especially if many sections contain no relevant data. CDC’s health-literacy guidance emphasizes accessibility and actionability, which can conflict with exhaustive uncertainty display. ([cdc.gov](https://www.cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/develop-test-materials.html))  
- **Open question:** Which packet sections require explicit absence reasons, and which can be omitted without implying “none.”  

### D. Revocation semantics

- **Option space:** Revocation can invalidate future link access, revoke future API/token access, mark a grant inactive, log revocation, or attempt downstream copy recall by notice/request. SMART Health Links covers inactive links and short-lived URLs; HIPAA demonstrates that revocation generally does not undo prior reliance/disclosure. ([docs.smarthealthit.org](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/spec/?utm_source=openai))  
- **Risk if revoke is presented as copy recall:** Users may believe already-exported PDFs, emails, screenshots, EHR uploads, or downloaded files are technically invalidated when external sources show that prior disclosures/copies commonly cannot be unwound automatically. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.508))  
- **Risk if revoke is presented only as link shutdown:** Users may underestimate that revocation can still be meaningful for future access, long-lived links, passcodes, dynamic manifests, and audit history. SMART Health Links explicitly supports no-longer-active link behavior. ([docs.smarthealthit.org](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/spec/?utm_source=openai))  
- **Open question:** Whether WellBe’s grant model distinguishes “controlled link share,” “download/export,” “recipient copied,” and “recipient acknowledged handling caveats.”  

### E. Audience and purpose limitation

- **Option space:** Audience can be a named clinician, clinic, care team, any holder of a link, or a user-selected recipient class; purpose can be appointment preparation, treatment discussion, second opinion, caregiver review, or personal archive. HIPAA and FHIR Consent both include recipient and purpose concepts, while SMART Health Links may be link-holder based unless layered with app policy. ([law.cornell.edu](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.508))  
- **Risk if audience is broad link-holder access:** A link model can be convenient but may not express a named audience unless the sharing application adds recipient controls. ([docs.smarthealthit.org](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/spec/?utm_source=openai))  
- **Risk if audience is strictly named:** Strict recipient binding may reduce practical shareability in clinical workflows where front-desk staff, nurses, or covering clinicians need access. FHIR Consent supports actors/classes, but enforcement depends on the access-control layer. ([hl7.org](https://hl7.org/fhir/r4/Consent.html))  
- **Open question:** Whether “audience” is enforced cryptographically, by authenticated access, by link possession/passcode, by policy labels, or by user-visible intent only.  

### F. C10 gate strictness

- **Option space:** The gate can block any diagnostic/treatment wording; allow neutral restatement of source diagnoses already present in records; allow patient-authored concerns/questions; or allow clinician-facing summaries with clear basis and limitations. FDA’s CDS guidance distinguishes summaries/options from directives and emphasizes independent review of basis. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/step-6-software-function-intended-provide-clinical-decision-support))  
- **Risk if too permissive:** Generated text may drift into diagnosis, treatment direction, risk scoring, urgency escalation, or time-critical alarm language. FDA’s examples identify these as sensitive categories. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/step-6-software-function-intended-provide-clinical-decision-support))  
- **Risk if too restrictive:** The packet may fail to communicate clinically useful, source-backed facts such as “source record lists asthma” or “patient reports chest tightness after exertion,” even when those are not new diagnoses. ([fda.gov](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/step-6-software-function-intended-provide-clinical-decision-support))  
- **Open question:** How C10 distinguishes: direct source fact, patient-reported observation, generated synthesis, generated inference, diagnostic label from source record, and new diagnostic label generated by AI.  

### G. Bias and equity review

- **Option space:** Bias checks can focus on language, source coverage, missing-data warnings, differential uncertainty, or model-output evaluation. WHO and NIST both identify bias/fairness, inclusiveness, transparency, and safety as AI-health governance concerns. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-06-2021-who-issues-first-global-report-on-ai-in-health-and-six-guiding-principles-for-its-design-and-use))  
- **Risk if limited to banned words:** Bias can arise from missing data, overconfidence, inappropriate inference, or unequal source quality, not only from wording. WHO specifically notes that systems trained primarily on high-income-country data may not perform well in other settings. ([who.int](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-06-2021-who-issues-first-global-report-on-ai-in-health-and-six-guiding-principles-for-its-design-and-use))  
- **Risk if bias review is too broad for MVP:** A broad fairness program can delay packet generation if not scoped to the concrete output risks at share time. NIST notes that AI trustworthiness characteristics involve tradeoffs and may vary by setting. ([nist.gov](https://www.nist.gov/node/1674681))  
- **Open question:** Which bias checks are mandatory pre-share gates versus post-hoc monitoring/audit signals.

## Approaches considered

_Grounded only in the recorded research above (IPS, FHIR Provenance/Consent/Composition/DocumentReference, SMART Health Links, HIPAA §164.508, FDA CDS, WHO, NIST, CDC, AHRQ, OpenNotes)._

**Q1 — Packet composition + source-linking**
- **A1a. IPS-style core summary:** mirror IPS minimum sections (allergies, medications, problems) + relevant extras. Pro: aligns with an established cross-care summary expectation. Con: skews toward a clinical record rather than a patient-prep artifact; risks stale/over-broad inclusion (IPS notes not all lifetime data is relevant).
- **A1b. Visit-prep-only artifact:** questions, goals, symptoms, current meds (AHRQ QuestionBuilder / OpenNotes OurNotes pattern). Pro: clearly patient-authored, non-diagnostic, low blast radius. Con: may omit allergies/meds/problems a clinician expects.
- **A1c. Two-layer packet:** a patient-prep layer (questions/goals/observations) + an optional source-backed summary layer, with claim-level provenance and explicit absence semantics (IPS `emptyReason`/known-absence). Pro: combines both patterns; preserves "no orphan claims" including no implied "none" from missing data. Con: highest implementation complexity (claim-level citation UX).

**Q2 — Scoped share/export + revocation**
- **A2a. Link-holder share (SMART Health Links pattern):** time-boxed, passcode-protected link; inactive link returns 404. Pro: concrete, proven, convenient. Con: audience is link-holder, not a named recipient, unless layered with recipient controls.
- **A2b. Named-audience grant (FHIR Consent / HIPAA §164.508 fields):** recipient, purpose, info scope, expiration, revocation notice, redisclosure warning. Pro: explicit, least-permission, maps cleanly to C1 grant fields. Con: strict recipient binding can reduce real-world shareability (front-desk/covering clinician).
- **A2c. Two-state model:** distinguish "controlled link share" (revocable future access) from "export/copy" (cannot be unwound), with revocation honestly scoped to future access + audit, never presented as copy-recall (HIPAA reliance exception; SHL inactive-link). Pro: honest, calm, matches legal reality. Con: requires clear UI to convey that exported copies persist.

**Q3 — C10 gating of generated text**
- **A3a. Banned-word/diagnosis blocklist:** simplest gate. Pro: cheap. Con: WHO/NIST note bias/overconfidence/missing-data harms aren't only wording; too weak alone.
- **A3b. Source-provenance gate (FDA "basis for independent review"):** every shareable claim must map to source evidence; block unsupported conclusions; classify each statement (direct source fact / patient-reported observation / generated synthesis / generated inference / source-record diagnosis / new AI diagnostic label) and block the last category. Pro: enforces "no orphan claims" + do-not-diagnose precisely; supports independent review. Con: needs the claim-classification + provenance plumbing from Q1c.
- **A3c. Layered gate:** A3b + calm/plain-language check (CDC) + a bias/equity pre-share subset (WHO/NIST) with the rest as post-hoc audit. Pro: most complete; scopes bias work to share-time risk. Con: largest gate to build for MVP.

## Decision

_Approved by user 2026-06-17._

**Q1:** Adopt **A1c (two-layer packet)** — a patient-prep layer plus an optional, user-selectable source-backed summary layer, with **claim-level** source links and **explicit absence semantics** (no silent omission that implies "none"). Required fields mean "include if known, else show an explicit absence reason"; user may deselect, but deselection is visibly marked, not silently dropped.

**Q2:** Adopt **A2b + A2c** — the grant object carries named recipient, purpose, info scope, and expiration (HIPAA/FHIR-Consent field set), shared via a time-boxed, passcode-protected, revocable link (SHL pattern). Revocation invalidates **future** link access and is audit-logged; the UI states plainly that already-exported/downloaded copies cannot be recalled. "Export/copy" is a distinct, clearly-warned state from "controlled link share."

**Q3:** Adopt **A3b for MVP, with A3c as the target** — C10 blocks any shareable claim not mapped to source evidence and any new AI-generated diagnostic/treatment/risk/alarm language, using a per-statement classification; a calm/plain-language check is included at share time; broader bias/equity checks start as post-hoc audit and graduate to pre-share gates over time.

## Trade-offs accepted

- **Implementation complexity.** Claim-level provenance + per-statement C10 classification (Q1c/Q3b) is materially harder than packet-level provenance or a banned-word gate. We accept this cost because "no orphan claims" and do-not-diagnose are core guarantees, not nice-to-haves.
- **Shareability friction.** Named-recipient grants are stricter than open link-holder sharing; covering clinicians/front-desk may need an explicit re-share. We accept this for honest, scoped audience semantics.
- **Honest-but-limited revocation.** Revocation only stops future access; we will not pretend exported copies can be recalled. We accept that the UI must clearly communicate this rather than offer false reassurance.
- **Phased bias review.** Bias/equity checks start as post-hoc audit, not pre-share gates, for MVP. We accept residual bias-exposure risk in exchange for shipping, and commit to graduating high-risk checks to pre-share over time.

## Implementation notes

- **Components:** C7 (thread→packet composition), C5 (claim-level provenance links), C1 (grant object + revocation log), C10 (pre-share gate). New C13 endpoint(s) for generate + share/export.
- **Packet model:** two layers — (1) patient-prep (questions, goals, observations; clearly patient-authored) and (2) optional source-backed summary. Every summary statement carries a source link; classify each as direct-source-fact / patient-reported / generated-synthesis / generated-inference / source-record-diagnosis / new-AI-diagnosis. Block the last category at the gate. Use explicit absence reasons (IPS `emptyReason`/known-absence) — never silently omit in a way that implies "none". Deselected fields are visibly marked, not dropped.
- **Grant object (C1):** recipient (named), purpose, info scope, expiration; revocation flips future access + writes an audit entry. Delivery via time-boxed, passcode-protected, revocable link (SMART Health Links pattern); inactive link returns 404. "Export/copy" is a distinct, clearly-warned state.
- **C10 gate:** runs before any packet can be shared/exported; enforces source-provenance + do-not-diagnose per-statement classification + calm/plain-language check at share time. Bias/equity audit logged post-hoc initially.
- **Unblocks:** WEL-68 (Visit Packet generator) and the `prepare/` UI; relates WEL-30.

# Track A: User-controlled clinician Visit Packet - decision-neutral research brief

This brief is decision-neutral. It inventories external standards, patterns, and limits relevant to a user-controlled, source-linked clinician Visit Packet. It does not choose a packet architecture, consent policy, revocation meaning, or safety-gate policy.

## External patterns to examine

### 1. Patient-facing and clinical-summary content patterns

**International Patient Summary (IPS) pattern.** The HL7 FHIR International Patient Summary (IPS) Implementation Guide frames a summary as an extract of essential healthcare information that is minimal, non-exhaustive, specialty-agnostic, and condition-independent, with content intended to support safe care across settings and jurisdictions ([HL7 FHIR IPS](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/)). The IPS structure includes required or recommended summary sections such as problems, allergies, medications, immunizations, diagnostic results, procedures, devices, advance directives, alerts, functional status, past problems, pregnancy history/status, plan of care, social history, and vital signs; it also includes a "Patient Story" section for what matters to the person, needs, strengths, values, concerns, and preferences ([HL7 IPS structure](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/STU2/Structure-of-the-International-Patient-Summary.html)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: IPS is an established clinical-summary structure for a concise, cross-setting health summary. It is not specifically a patient-authored visit packet, but it supplies an external vocabulary for what clinicians may expect in a portable health summary and for distinguishing required, recommended, and optional sections. It also includes explicit handling for missing/empty section content, which is relevant to avoiding silent omissions in a generated packet ([HL7 IPS structure](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/STU2/Structure-of-the-International-Patient-Summary.html)).

**C-CDA and patient-generated document header pattern.** HL7 C-CDA provides a document-based clinical communication pattern for structured notes, referrals, continuity of care, and care plans, with section templates and entry templates used to organize narrative plus computable data ([HL7 C-CDA Implementation Guide](https://build.fhir.org/ig/HL7/CDA-ccda/)). HL7 also defines a US Realm header pattern for patient-generated documents; the examples show explicit roles such as author, author device, custodian, informant, information recipient, legal authenticator, and record target ([HL7 C-CDA patient-generated document examples](https://www.hl7.org/cda/us/ccda/StructureDefinition-USRealmHeaderforPatientGeneratedDocument-examples.html)). The related HL7 product brief describes the patient-generated document header as guidance for documents authored by patients, patient representatives, or devices ([HL7 patient-generated document header product brief](https://dev1.hl7.org/implement/standards/product_brief.cfm?product_id=316)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: C-CDA is US-centric and CDA-specific, but it is a direct precedent for representing a document as patient-generated, separating patient/device/software authorship from clinical authorship, and preserving recipients/custodians in metadata.

**Patient visit-preparation pattern.** Patient-facing sources emphasize appointment preparation through a small number of important questions, symptom details, medication lists, and clear communication of goals and concerns. AHRQ's Question Builder helps patients and caregivers prepare for medical appointments and create questions for different visit types ([AHRQ Question Builder](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/question-builder/index.html), [AHRQ Question Builder online](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/question-builder/online.html)). NHS guidance asks patients to write down two or three important questions, bring a list of medicines including vitamins and supplements, and record symptom details such as when symptoms started and what makes them better or worse ([NHS - what to ask your doctor](https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/gps/what-to-ask-your-doctor/)). MedlinePlus and the National Institute on Aging similarly emphasize preparing concerns, describing symptoms honestly, and telling the doctor about medicines, habits, and health changes ([MedlinePlus - making the most of a doctor visit](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000860.htm), [NIA - what to tell your doctor](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/medical-care-and-appointments/what-do-i-need-tell-doctor)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: these sources support a visit-focused summary pattern that is not only a broad longitudinal record, but also an agenda: what changed, what the patient wants to discuss, what is pending, what questions remain, and what would make the encounter useful.

**OurNotes / co-generated note pattern.** OpenNotes' OurNotes model asks patients to compose a short interval history and identify two or three visit goals or questions before a visit, using the subjective portion of the SOAP note as a patient-authored contribution ([OpenNotes OurNotes](https://www.opennotes.org/ournotes/)). Published evaluation literature describes OurNotes as a patient co-generated note workflow through secure portals ([JMIR - OurNotes mixed-methods evaluation](https://www.jmir.org/2021/11/e29951)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: OurNotes is a close external pattern for patient-authored pre-visit information that remains clinically bounded: interval history, goals, and questions rather than diagnosis or treatment conclusions.

### 2. FHIR document, snapshot, and source-indexing patterns

**FHIR Document / Composition pattern.** FHIR documents use a Bundle of type `document`, with a `Composition` as the first resource and supporting resources referenced from it; the document has both human-readable and machine-processable portions ([FHIR Documents](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documents.html)). FHIR describes `Composition` as a set of healthcare-related information assembled into a single logical package and the basis for immutable document bundles with attested narrative ([FHIR Composition](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/composition.html)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: this pattern is relevant to a Visit Packet as a generated point-in-time packet assembled from one or more living Health Threads. It exposes a structural distinction between the live thread state and the shared/exported document snapshot.

**FHIR DocumentReference pattern.** `DocumentReference` provides metadata about a document so it can be discovered and managed, while the referenced content may be stored inline or through an external reference ([FHIR DocumentReference](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documentreference.html)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: this pattern is relevant for raw source documents such as lab PDFs, portal notes, imaging reports, referral letters, user-uploaded files, or packet exports. It supports document-level source indexing, but not necessarily sentence-level proof.

### 3. Provenance and citation patterns

**FHIR Provenance pattern.** FHIR `Provenance` records entities, agents, and activities involved in producing or influencing a resource; it is intended to support authenticity, trust, reproducibility, and assessment of quality or reliability ([FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html)). FHIR aligns this model with W3C PROV and supports references to target resources, agents, activities, and source entities, including entity roles such as source, quotation, revision, and instantiation ([FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html)).

**W3C PROV pattern.** W3C PROV is a general provenance model for representing entities, activities, and agents involved in producing data or things, with the goal of interoperable provenance exchange ([W3C PROV overview](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-overview/)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: these models provide an external vocabulary for "this packet statement came from this source, through this generation activity, at this time, by these agents." They do not, by themselves, guarantee that every natural-language sentence in a generated packet is individually source-linked; that requires a claim-level implementation pattern on top of resource-level provenance.

### 4. Consent, scoped sharing, and revocation patterns

**FHIR Consent pattern.** FHIR `Consent` represents a healthcare consumer's or third party's choices to permit or deny actions by recipients or roles, for purposes, time periods, and data scopes ([FHIR Consent](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/consent.html)). The resource explicitly models context dimensions such as who grants consent, what data is covered, where boundaries apply, when it was issued and valid, how data may be used, and whom it applies to; provisions can express permit/deny rules, exceptions, purpose, security label, document type, resource type, and specific data ([FHIR Consent](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/consent.html)).

**FHIR security/privacy and audit pattern.** FHIR's security and privacy module presents building blocks rather than a single mandatory security architecture; it covers access control, permissions through Consent, logging with AuditEvent and Provenance, OAuth/SMART usage, token expiration, introspection/revocation, and patient-facing accounting of disclosures where feasible ([FHIR Security and Privacy module](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/secpriv-module.html)).

**IHE Privacy Consent on FHIR (PCF) pattern.** IHE PCF applies FHIR consent and token flows to access control in document-sharing health information exchange environments. Its own scope notes that it does not cover all possible consent use cases ([IHE ITI PCF](https://profiles.ihe.net/ITI/PCF/)).

**EHR-S Function Model privacy-consent pattern.** HL7's EHR System Functional Model describes patient-specific privacy consent directives, including consents for a specific disclosure, a period of time, or until explicitly revoked; it also frames consent enforcement through access control, secure routing, segmentation, and disclosure accounting ([HL7 EHR-S FM AS.2.6](https://build.fhir.org/ig/HL7/ehrsfm-ig/en/Requirements-EHRSFMR2-AS.2.6.html)).

**OAuth / UMA grant pattern.** OAuth token revocation defines a revocation endpoint for clients to notify an authorization server that a token is no longer needed, invalidating the token and, where applicable, other tokens based on the same grant ([RFC 7009 - OAuth 2.0 Token Revocation](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc7009)). UMA 2.0 builds on OAuth to allow resource owners to control access to protected resources by requesting parties and clients, with an authorization server managing policies and grants over time ([Kantara UMA 2.0 Grant for OAuth 2.0 Authorization](https://docs.kantarainitiative.org/uma/wg/uma-core-2.0-20.html)). Rich Authorization Requests provide a pattern for expressing more detailed authorization information about protected resources and actions ([RFC 9396 - OAuth 2.0 Rich Authorization Requests](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9396.html)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: these standards describe live-access control patterns for audience, purpose, data scope, actions, duration, and revocation of future access. They also expose a boundary: token revocation stops or constrains future access through the controlled service, but does not recall a copy that a recipient has already downloaded or exported.

**SMART Health Links and SMART Health Cards pattern.** SMART Health Links describe sharing FHIR data through static or dynamic links, QR codes, passcodes, limited-time or one-time links, and links to FHIR APIs ([SMART Health Links design](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/design/)). SMART Health Cards are signed verifiable credentials designed for portable disclosure; the specification explicitly discusses data minimization and notes that if individual revocation is not possible, issuers may need to revoke an issuing key and let users obtain new cards ([SMART Health Cards specification](https://spec.smarthealth.cards/)). W3C's Bitstring Status List provides a privacy-preserving status mechanism for revocation or suspension of verifiable credentials, dependent on verifiers checking status ([W3C Bitstring Status List v1.0](https://www.w3.org/TR/vc-bitstring-status-list/)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: link-based and credential-based export patterns differ in revocation semantics. Dynamic links can stop future fetches; offline signed artifacts can be marked invalid only if verification checks status or key state, and copies may still exist.

**Legal revocation analogs.** HIPAA authorization revocation can generally occur in writing, but is not effective to the extent a covered entity has already taken action in reliance on the authorization ([HHS HIPAA FAQ on revocation](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/474/can-an-individual-revoke-his-or-her-authorization/index.html), [45 CFR 164.508](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.508)). GDPR consent withdrawal does not affect the lawfulness of processing before withdrawal ([GDPR Article 7](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-7-gdpr/)); GDPR erasure may apply in some circumstances but has exceptions, including where processing is required by law or for certain health/public-health purposes ([GDPR Article 17](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/), [ICO guidance on right to erasure](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-erasure/?id=15)). The FTC's mobile health apps tool notes that HIPAA does not apply to health information maintained in a mobile app if the app developer is not a covered entity or business associate, even if the data originated from a covered entity ([FTC Mobile Health Apps Interactive Tool](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/mobile-health-apps-interactive-tool)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: these sources do not determine WellBe's legal status, but they provide external analogs for separating withdrawal of future permission from undoing already-completed disclosures or copies.

### 5. Safety, non-diagnostic output, and governance patterns

**AI risk-management pattern.** NIST's AI Risk Management Framework describes trustworthy AI characteristics including validity and reliability, safety, security and resilience, accountability and transparency, explainability and interpretability, privacy enhancement, and fairness with harmful bias managed ([NIST AI RMF characteristics](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/)).

**AI for health governance pattern.** WHO's ethics and governance guidance for AI in health emphasizes human rights, accountability, and governance for health workers, communities, and individuals ([WHO Ethics and Governance of AI for Health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)). WHO's regulatory considerations for AI in health discuss risk-benefit assessment, performance evaluation, monitoring, and stakeholder responsibilities, while stating that the publication is not itself a regulatory framework or formal guidance ([WHO Regulatory considerations on AI for health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240078871)).

**Clinical decision support boundary pattern.** FDA's clinical decision support software page describes guidance on when CDS software is or is not considered a device under section 520(o)(1)(E) of the FD&C Act, including patient/caregiver-facing and healthcare-professional-facing examples ([FDA Clinical Decision Support Software guidance page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software)).

Relevance to the Visit Packet: these sources frame safety gating as a combination of intended-use control, transparency, human reviewability, provenance, validation, monitoring, and bias management. They do not prescribe a particular Visit Packet gate contract.

## Decision-neutral findings

### Decision question 1: Composition and source-linking

**Clinical-summary structures provide a broad content vocabulary, while visit-prep sources provide a focused encounter vocabulary.** IPS supplies a broad health-summary pattern: problems, allergies, medications, immunizations, diagnostic results, procedures, devices, advance directives, alerts, functional status, past problems, patient story, plan of care, social history, and vital signs ([HL7 IPS structure](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/STU2/Structure-of-the-International-Patient-Summary.html)). Patient visit-preparation sources emphasize a narrower encounter pattern: key questions, medication/supplement list, symptom onset and modifiers, changes since the last visit, concerns, and goals for the encounter ([AHRQ Question Builder](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/question-builder/index.html), [NHS - what to ask your doctor](https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/gps/what-to-ask-your-doctor/), [MedlinePlus - making the most of a doctor visit](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000860.htm)). OurNotes is especially relevant because it frames patient-authored pre-visit input as interval history plus two or three goals/questions, rather than a diagnosis or treatment plan ([OpenNotes OurNotes](https://www.opennotes.org/ournotes/)).

**Mapping to C7 Health Thread Engine.** The external sources map to several packet-content categories that may be assembled from one or more Health Threads: patient-stated concern, interval history, symptom timeline, baseline changes, medications/supplements, test results, clinician contacts, referrals, pending items, corrections, and open questions. IPS supplies longitudinal health-summary categories; AHRQ/NHS/MedlinePlus/OurNotes supply visit-agenda categories. The standards do not settle how much of a Health Thread is included, how multiple threads are merged, or how to prioritize the packet when a user has many unresolved concerns.

**Patient story and patient-authored content are established categories, but they need labeling.** IPS includes a Patient Story section for the person's needs, strengths, values, concerns, and preferences ([HL7 IPS structure](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/STU2/Structure-of-the-International-Patient-Summary.html)). C-CDA patient-generated document patterns explicitly distinguish a patient-generated document header and metadata roles such as author, author device, custodian, informant, and recipient ([HL7 C-CDA patient-generated document examples](https://www.hl7.org/cda/us/ccda/StructureDefinition-USRealmHeaderforPatientGeneratedDocument-examples.html)). These patterns support separating patient-authored statements from imported clinical records and AI-generated summaries. They do not define a single UX or data model for this separation.

**FHIR document patterns distinguish a generated packet snapshot from live thread state.** A FHIR document Bundle is a point-in-time package beginning with a Composition and followed by referenced supporting resources ([FHIR Documents](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documents.html)). FHIR Composition is used for assembled document content and can represent immutable document bundles ([FHIR Composition](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/composition.html)). For WellBe, this is evidence that an exported or shared packet can be modeled as a generated artifact separate from living Health Threads. The sources do not decide whether shared clinician access should always see the frozen packet, the live underlying thread, or a hybrid of frozen summary plus live source links.

**DocumentReference and Provenance cover source indexing and lineage, but not automatic sentence-level proof.** DocumentReference can index source documents and referenced content ([FHIR DocumentReference](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documentreference.html)). FHIR Provenance can record agents, entities, and activities involved in generating or influencing a resource, including source and quotation relationships ([FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html)). W3C PROV provides the general entity/activity/agent model behind provenance exchange ([W3C PROV overview](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-overview/)). These sources establish a pattern for linking packet content to raw sources, versioned resources, and generation events. They do not, by themselves, enforce WellBe's "no orphan claims" requirement at the level of each natural-language claim.

**Mapping to C5 Evidence & Provenance Service.** The evidence model implied by the sources has at least four separable layers: raw source material, normalized resources or extracted facts, generated packet claims, and the generation/revision activity that connected them. FHIR Provenance and W3C PROV provide vocabulary for source entity, agent, activity, target, and time ([FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html), [W3C PROV overview](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-overview/)). A WellBe-specific no-orphan-claim rule would need to determine the required granularity of source links: packet-level, section-level, bullet-level, sentence-level, or structured-claim-level.

**Missing-information handling is a content-design issue, not only a display issue.** IPS explicitly includes patterns for no-information and no-entry cases in sections such as medication lists ([HL7 IPS structure](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/STU2/Structure-of-the-International-Patient-Summary.html)). In a Visit Packet, this pattern matters because absence of evidence can be misread as evidence of absence. The sources support making missing, unknown, not-yet-imported, and explicitly negative information distinguishable; they do not define which missingness states WellBe should expose.

**Mapping to C10 Safety & Governance Gate.** Source-linking intersects safety because generated text can overstate, infer, or diagnose from incomplete sources. NIST's AI RMF identifies validity, reliability, safety, transparency, explainability, and bias management as trustworthy AI characteristics ([NIST AI RMF characteristics](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/)). WHO's AI-for-health guidance similarly emphasizes governance, accountability, and ethics in health AI ([WHO Ethics and Governance of AI for Health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)). For the packet, these sources support treating provenance completeness, uncertainty labeling, and non-diagnostic framing as safety-relevant checks rather than purely formatting concerns.

### Decision question 2: Scoped share/export and revocation

**FHIR Consent provides a structured vocabulary for grant scope.** FHIR Consent models who grants consent, what data is covered, where boundaries and accountability apply, when the consent is issued and valid, how data may be used, and whom the consent applies to; provisions can express permit/deny rules and exceptions by purpose, actor, period, document type, resource type, security label, and data reference ([FHIR Consent](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/consent.html)). This maps directly to Visit Packet dimensions: grantor, recipient, purpose, duration, actions, packet version, underlying thread/source scope, and policy basis. The standard does not decide which dimensions must be surfaced to the user or how granular a user-facing grant should be.

**Security standards distinguish policy expression from enforcement.** FHIR's security/privacy module describes OAuth/SMART, Consent, AuditEvent, Provenance, token expiration, token introspection, and token revocation as building blocks, while stating that FHIR does not mandate a single security approach ([FHIR Security and Privacy module](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/secpriv-module.html)). IHE PCF combines FHIR consent and token flows for document-sharing HIE access but limits its own scope to a subset of consent use cases ([IHE ITI PCF](https://profiles.ihe.net/ITI/PCF/)). The EHR-S Functional Model links consent directives to access control, segmentation, secure routing, and disclosure accounting ([HL7 EHR-S FM AS.2.6](https://build.fhir.org/ig/HL7/ehrsfm-ig/en/Requirements-EHRSFMR2-AS.2.6.html)). These sources frame the C1 Trust & Consent Service as both a grant-record service and an enforcement/audit coordination point.

**OAuth, UMA, and rich authorization patterns cover live access, not copy recall.** OAuth token revocation invalidates a token and potentially related tokens derived from the same grant ([RFC 7009 - OAuth 2.0 Token Revocation](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc7009)). UMA 2.0 supports resource-owner controlled access to protected resources through an authorization server over time ([Kantara UMA 2.0 Grant for OAuth 2.0 Authorization](https://docs.kantarainitiative.org/uma/wg/uma-core-2.0-20.html)). Rich Authorization Requests provide a way to represent detailed authorization parameters about protected resources and actions ([RFC 9396 - OAuth 2.0 Rich Authorization Requests](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9396.html)). These patterns are evidence for audience/purpose/duration/scope grants on live access. They do not establish a mechanism to invalidate a copy already viewed, downloaded, printed, screenshotted, forwarded, or imported into another system.

**SMART link and credential patterns expose different revocation semantics.** SMART Health Links include static and dynamic sharing models, limited-time or one-time QR codes, passcodes, encrypted storage, and API-backed access ([SMART Health Links design](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/design/)). SMART Health Cards are portable signed credentials and discuss the limitation that if individual revocation is not possible, revoking an issuer key and reissuing cards may be the fallback ([SMART Health Cards specification](https://spec.smarthealth.cards/)). W3C Bitstring Status List provides a revocation/suspension status mechanism for verifiable credentials, but effectiveness depends on verifier status checking ([W3C Bitstring Status List v1.0](https://www.w3.org/TR/vc-bitstring-status-list/)). For a Visit Packet, this evidence distinguishes at least three share forms: live service access, link-based document retrieval, and offline export/credential. Each form has different revocation strength.

**Legal analogs distinguish future permission from past action.** HIPAA allows revocation of authorization in writing, but revocation is not effective to the extent a covered entity has already acted in reliance on the authorization ([HHS HIPAA FAQ on revocation](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/474/can-an-individual-revoke-his-or-her-authorization/index.html), [45 CFR 164.508](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.508)). GDPR states that withdrawal of consent does not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before withdrawal ([GDPR Article 7](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-7-gdpr/)). GDPR erasure can apply where conditions are met, but Article 17 and regulator guidance include exceptions, including legal obligations and some health-related contexts ([GDPR Article 17](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/), [ICO guidance on right to erasure](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-erasure/?id=15)). These sources do not determine WellBe's compliance obligations, but they support a factual distinction between revoking future access and undoing completed disclosures.

**Mapping to C1 Trust & Consent Service.** The evidence supports modeling a share grant as more than a URL: it can include grantor, grantee, purpose, permitted actions, packet/resource scope, duration, revocation status, audit trail, and source policy. The sources also show that C1 cannot truthfully promise universal recall of exported/downloaded data using token revocation alone. Any revocation model has to state what is technically enforced inside WellBe-controlled channels and what remains outside WellBe's control after export.

**Mapping to C5 and C7.** Consent scope can be expressed at different object levels: packet version, Health Thread, data category, source document, date range, or individual claim. FHIR Consent can reference document type, resource type, and specific data ([FHIR Consent](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/consent.html)); FHIR Documents and Composition can package supporting resources in a document snapshot ([FHIR Documents](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documents.html)). The standards leave the granularity choice open.

**Mapping to C10.** Because a packet can leave the personal boundary when shared or exported, the pre-share safety gate has irreversible consequences. Token expiration or later revocation may prevent future access, but cannot guarantee correction of already-used text. This finding is grounded in the difference between access-token revocation semantics and legal non-retroactivity/reliance limits ([RFC 7009 - OAuth 2.0 Token Revocation](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc7009), [HHS HIPAA FAQ on revocation](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/474/can-an-individual-revoke-his-or-her-authorization/index.html), [GDPR Article 7](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-7-gdpr/)).

### Decision question 3: Safety gating before clinician sharing

**External AI governance sources frame gating as validation, transparency, accountability, and bias management.** NIST identifies trustworthy AI characteristics including validity and reliability, safety, accountability and transparency, explainability and interpretability, privacy, and fairness with harmful bias managed ([NIST AI RMF characteristics](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/)). WHO's AI-for-health guidance emphasizes ethics, human rights, accountability, and governance in health settings ([WHO Ethics and Governance of AI for Health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)); WHO's regulatory considerations include risk-benefit assessment, evaluation, monitoring, and stakeholder responsibilities ([WHO Regulatory considerations on AI for health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240078871)). For WellBe, these sources map to C10 checks such as source completeness, intended-use boundaries, uncertainty handling, calm language, auditability, and bias-sensitive summarization.

**Non-diagnostic framing is a safety and intended-use boundary.** FDA's CDS guidance page concerns when clinical decision support software falls inside or outside medical-device regulation, including patient/caregiver and clinician examples ([FDA Clinical Decision Support Software guidance page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software)). The Visit Packet context is not a clinician decision-support module or diagnosis engine; the researched pattern space supports framing generated text as a patient-controlled communication aid: reported symptoms, observed changes, sourced results, pending items, and questions to discuss. The evidence does not define the exact language policy, blocklist, or classifier threshold for enforcing that boundary.

**Safety gating intersects provenance.** A generated summary that says "labs were normal," "symptoms worsened," or "referral is pending" needs traceability to a lab result, user entry, message, visit note, or thread state. FHIR Provenance provides a way to record the activity and source entities behind generated resources ([FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html)); DocumentReference provides source-document metadata ([FHIR DocumentReference](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documentreference.html)). C10 can therefore treat unsupported claims as a safety failure, a provenance failure, or both. The standards do not decide whether patient-reported unsourced statements are allowed if clearly labeled as patient-reported.

**Safety gating also intersects missingness and conflicts.** IPS's empty/no-information patterns show that structured summaries should distinguish no data, no known data, and absent entries rather than silently omitting content ([HL7 IPS structure](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/STU2/Structure-of-the-International-Patient-Summary.html)). For a Visit Packet, this matters when sources conflict, when a result is missing, or when a thread has open corrections. The sources support making uncertainty visible; they do not prescribe whether uncertainty should block sharing, require user review, or appear in the packet.

**Bias and equity checks are part of the gate option space.** NIST explicitly includes fairness and harmful-bias management in trustworthy AI characteristics ([NIST AI RMF characteristics](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/)). IPS's Patient Story section includes needs, strengths, values, concerns, preferences, and what matters to the person ([HL7 IPS structure](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/STU2/Structure-of-the-International-Patient-Summary.html)). In the Visit Packet context, this maps to possible checks for stigmatizing language, unsupported behavioral assumptions, demographic assumptions, minimization of symptoms, inaccessible language, and omission of patient goals or communication needs. The evidence does not set a single taxonomy or passing threshold.

**Human reviewability is a recurring governance theme.** NIST and WHO both emphasize transparency, accountability, and explainability/interpretability for AI systems ([NIST AI RMF characteristics](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/), [WHO Ethics and Governance of AI for Health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)). FHIR document and provenance patterns support traceability from a packet back to sources and generation events ([FHIR Documents](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documents.html), [FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html)). For a user-controlled Visit Packet, the evidence supports a distinction between generated text, user edits, and imported clinical data. It does not determine whether user approval alone is sufficient after C10 gating, whether clinician-facing recipients should see the full provenance UI, or how much explanation is needed in the exported artifact.

## Tradeoffs and open questions

### 1. Packet content scope

**Option space:** a broad IPS-like health summary; a narrow visit-agenda packet; or a hybrid with a concise agenda plus relevant longitudinal context. IPS supports a broad essential-health summary ([HL7 FHIR IPS](https://www.hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/)); AHRQ/NHS/OurNotes support encounter-preparation content ([AHRQ Question Builder](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/question-builder/index.html), [NHS - what to ask your doctor](https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/gps/what-to-ask-your-doctor/), [OpenNotes OurNotes](https://www.opennotes.org/ournotes/)).

**Risk if too broad:** the packet may become long, noisy, or hard for clinicians to scan. **Risk if too narrow:** important context such as medication changes, prior results, referrals, or baseline changes may be omitted. **Open question:** what default composition rules distinguish essential, optional, excluded, and user-selected content for one thread versus multiple threads?

### 2. Claim-linking granularity

**Option space:** packet-level provenance, section-level citations, bullet/sentence-level citations, or structured atomic claims behind a rendered summary. FHIR Provenance supports resource-level lineage and source relationships ([FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html)); W3C PROV supplies a general entity/activity/agent model ([W3C PROV overview](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-overview/)).

**Risk if too coarse:** a packet may appear source-linked while individual derived claims remain unsupported or ambiguous. **Risk if too fine:** the packet may become visually cluttered, hard to maintain, or brittle when language is edited. **Open question:** what is the minimum citation unit that satisfies WellBe's no-orphan-claim rule while remaining readable to users and clinicians?

### 3. Patient-reported, imported, and AI-derived facts

**Option space:** visually separate patient-reported statements, imported clinical records, device-generated data, and AI-generated summaries; or combine them in one narrative with citations and labels. C-CDA patient-generated document headers distinguish patient, device, custodian, and recipient roles ([HL7 C-CDA patient-generated document examples](https://www.hl7.org/cda/us/ccda/StructureDefinition-USRealmHeaderforPatientGeneratedDocument-examples.html)); FHIR Provenance distinguishes agents and source entities ([FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html)).

**Risk if separated too aggressively:** the packet may become fragmented and hard to read. **Risk if not separated:** clinician recipients may mistake patient-authored or AI-composed text for clinician-authored facts. **Open question:** what labeling must survive export, print, screenshots, and EHR upload?

### 4. Missingness, normal results, and negative statements

**Option space:** include explicit empty/unknown/not imported/not asked/not applicable states; or omit empty sections for brevity. IPS includes explicit no-information/no-entry patterns for summary sections ([HL7 IPS structure](https://hl7.org/fhir/uv/ips/STU2/Structure-of-the-International-Patient-Summary.html)).

**Risk if explicit states are omitted:** absence may be misread as clinically negative evidence. **Risk if every unknown is shown:** the packet may look incomplete or alarm users unnecessarily. **Open question:** which missingness states are clinically useful in a Visit Packet and which should remain behind source detail views?

### 5. Snapshot versus live packet

**Option space:** immutable generated packet, dynamic live packet link, or immutable summary with live source attachments. FHIR Documents and Composition support document snapshots ([FHIR Documents](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documents.html), [FHIR Composition](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/composition.html)); SMART Health Links support static and dynamic sharing patterns ([SMART Health Links design](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/design/)).

**Risk with immutable snapshots:** shared copies can become stale after corrections, new results, or revoked consent. **Risk with live packets:** the recipient view may change after the user approved the original packet, complicating auditability and clinician reliance. **Open question:** when a Health Thread changes after sharing, is the prior packet marked stale, replaced, versioned, or left as a historical artifact?

### 6. Share scope and consent granularity

**Option space:** scope by packet version, Health Thread, source category, date range, document, individual fact, recipient identity, purpose, action, and duration. FHIR Consent supports provisions by purpose, actor, period, resource/document/data categories, and data references ([FHIR Consent](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/consent.html)).

**Risk if scope is coarse:** the user may overshare sensitive unrelated context. **Risk if scope is too granular:** the consent UX may be confusing, and clinicians may receive incomplete context. **Open question:** what consent dimensions are mandatory at share time, which are advanced controls, and which are implicit in the packet artifact itself?

### 7. Revocation semantics and user promises

**Option space:** revocation disables future WellBe-hosted access only; revocation also marks the packet version revoked and notifies prior recipients; revocation triggers a deletion request workflow; or revocation affects only future grants while exports remain outside control. OAuth token revocation covers token invalidation ([RFC 7009 - OAuth 2.0 Token Revocation](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc7009)); HIPAA and GDPR analogs distinguish withdrawal from undoing prior action or prior lawful processing ([HHS HIPAA FAQ on revocation](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/474/can-an-individual-revoke-his-or-her-authorization/index.html), [GDPR Article 7](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-7-gdpr/)).

**Risk if revocation is described too strongly:** users may believe copies are recalled when they are not. **Risk if described too weakly:** users may not trust scoped sharing even when live access can be technically revoked. **Open question:** what exact user-facing language defines revocation for live links, downloaded files, printed packets, screenshots, and recipient EHR imports?

### 8. Export format

**Option space:** human-readable PDF/Markdown, FHIR Bundle/Composition, C-CDA-like document, SMART Health Link, signed credential, or multiple parallel formats. FHIR Documents support structured document bundles ([FHIR Documents](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documents.html)); C-CDA supports clinical document exchange ([HL7 C-CDA Implementation Guide](https://build.fhir.org/ig/HL7/CDA-ccda/)); SMART Health Links support link-mediated FHIR sharing ([SMART Health Links design](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/design/)).

**Risk with human-only export:** easy to read but weak for structured provenance and downstream computation. **Risk with structured-only export:** interoperable in theory but less useful for immediate visit conversation. **Open question:** what source links, labels, version IDs, revocation notices, and safety disclaimers must be embedded in every export format?

### 9. Recipient identity and access assurance

**Option space:** user-entered email/link sharing, verified clinician identity, SMART/FHIR organization-mediated access, or recipient-specific grant plus passcode/PIN. SMART Health Links include passcodes and time-limited/one-time link concepts ([SMART Health Links design](https://docs.smarthealthit.org/smart-health-links/design/)); FHIR security guidance discusses OAuth/SMART and constrained scopes ([FHIR Security and Privacy module](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/secpriv-module.html)).

**Risk with low-friction sharing:** accidental disclosure to the wrong person or forwarding risk. **Risk with high-assurance recipient verification:** user friction and reduced use in real-world visits. **Open question:** what level of identity proofing is required for a clinician share compared with a user-downloaded export?

### 10. Safety-gate strictness

**Option space:** block any unsupported claim; allow patient-reported unsourced claims with explicit labels; block diagnostic/treatment wording; require user review after AI generation; require regenerated output after edits; or gate both generated text and exported rendering. NIST and WHO support safety, transparency, validation, accountability, and bias management as governance dimensions ([NIST AI RMF characteristics](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/), [WHO Ethics and Governance of AI for Health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)).

**Risk if too strict:** useful patient context may be blocked, delayed, or flattened. **Risk if too permissive:** the packet may include unsupported, alarming, biased, or diagnostic language before it leaves the user's control boundary. **Open question:** which gate failures are hard blocks, which are warnings requiring user edit, and which are allowed with labels?

### 11. Urgent or potentially serious content

**Option space:** remove panic language while preserving factual symptom reports; include a neutral safety notice; block sharing until emergency guidance is shown; or avoid triage logic entirely. WHO and NIST frame health AI risks through safety, risk management, and accountability ([WHO Regulatory considerations on AI for health](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240078871), [NIST AI RMF characteristics](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/)).

**Risk if urgent language is suppressed too much:** clinically important red flags may be hidden. **Risk if too alarmist:** the product may violate calm/never-alarm guardrails and increase user distress. **Open question:** how should C10 distinguish calm factual reporting of concerning symptoms from diagnostic or alarmist interpretation?

### 12. Corrections, stale packets, and recipient notification

**Option space:** corrections create a new packet version; corrections mark previous packets stale; corrections notify recipients with active grants; corrections do not affect prior exports. FHIR document snapshots and provenance can record versions and generation activity ([FHIR Documents](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/documents.html), [FHIR Provenance](https://hl7.org/fhir/R5/provenance.html)).

**Risk if prior packets are not marked:** clinicians may rely on outdated or corrected information. **Risk if automatic updates are too broad:** recipients may receive newly revealed information outside the original grant expectation. **Open question:** what is the relationship between Health Thread correction state, packet version state, grant scope, and recipient notification?

### 13. Audit logs and accounting of access

**Option space:** show the user grant history only; show access events; show recipient identity, time, IP/device, action, packet version, and purpose; or aggregate events for privacy/security. FHIR security guidance identifies AuditEvent as a way to record security- and privacy-relevant events and support accounting of disclosures where feasible ([FHIR Security and Privacy module](https://fhir.hl7.org/fhir/secpriv-module.html)).

**Risk if audit is minimal:** users may lack confidence that sharing is controlled. **Risk if audit is too detailed:** it may expose recipient operational details or create retention and privacy issues. **Open question:** what access events are recorded, retained, displayed to the user, and included in the revocation log?

### 14. Legal/regulatory classification

**Option space:** treat HIPAA, GDPR, consumer-health-app, and medical-device/CDS frameworks as separate applicability analyses rather than one universal rule. HHS, FTC, GDPR/ICO, and FDA sources show that obligations depend on actor status, jurisdiction, legal basis, intended use, and context ([FTC Mobile Health Apps Interactive Tool](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/mobile-health-apps-interactive-tool), [HHS HIPAA FAQ on revocation](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/474/can-an-individual-revoke-his-or-her-authorization/index.html), [GDPR Article 7](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-7-gdpr/), [FDA Clinical Decision Support Software guidance page](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software)).

**Risk if treated as purely technical:** legal limits on consent, erasure, clinical software, and disclosure may be missed. **Risk if treated as one-size-fits-all law:** the product may overfit to one jurisdiction or covered-entity model. **Open question:** which jurisdictions and actor roles are in scope for the initial decision record, and which are deferred to legal/regulatory review?
